Systems in service

Seven built, three switched on right now. Open any row for the chain it actually executes — trigger, steps, gates, and the one decision that makes it work.

  1. A Vapi voice agent answers the call, collects what the caller needs, and calls one webhook tool — save_and_book — once, at the end of the call. Everything after that is n8n.

    1. Vapi tool callWebhook · POST

      Riya calls her one tool at the end of the conversation, carrying the caller's goal, level, hours a week and chosen slot.

    2. Read the callCode

      Unpacks the tool-call arguments into one flat record the rest of the chain can read.

    3. Save the goalGoogle Sheets · append

      Writes the row.

    4. Book the sessionGoogle Calendar · create

      Creates the event in the slot the caller picked.

    5. Reply to RiyaRespond to Webhook

      Returns the result to Vapi in about a second — while the call is still open and the caller is still listening.

    6. CoachAI Agent · Gemini + output parser

      Writes a personalised plan in a fixed format. Runs after the reply has already gone.

    7. Send the planGmail

      Emails it to the caller.

    The decision that makes it work. Generation takes seconds, and a caller on the phone will not wait seconds. The webhook response is ordered before the AI steps, so the caller hears a confirmation immediately and the written plan lands in their inbox a moment later.

    Built for a skill-coaching desk. The same shape fits clinics, salons, studios — any business that runs on bookings.

  2. Applications arrive as whatever people send — a message, a form, a mail. What comes out is a scored, reasoned shortlist with interviews already on the calendar.

    1. Parse the applicationCode

      Free text in, structured record out: years of experience, current role, skills, expectation, notice period, location.

    2. Score against the roleRubric · 100 points

      Fixed weights, same for everyone: must-have skills 40 · experience band 20 · salary against budget 15 · good-to-have 10 · already in the city 8 · notice period 7.

    3. Log the verdictScreening log

      Every candidate leaves with a score, a verdict and a written reason — shortlisted, talent pool, or not progressing. Nothing is a silent no.

    4. Bias audit31 terms checked

      Thirty-one protected-attribute terms swept across the job description and every panel note. Scoring touches five things only: skills, years, salary against budget, location, notice.

    5. Book the interviewGoogle Calendar · create

      Fixed slots, 45 minutes, three days out — with the briefing inside the invite: the score, one strength to confirm, one thing to probe.

    The decision that makes it work. A candidate scored 57 against the senior role scored 87 against the mid-band one. Same person, same skills — the band was wrong, not the candidate. The pipeline re-matches against open roles before it rejects anyone, which is the difference between a filter and a recruiter.

    Client, candidates and salary bands are withheld here. The rubric, the weights and the re-match are the real ones.

  3. Five workflows around one shared brain. A lead arrives, gets screened, scored and answered with a grounded draft — and nothing reaches a customer without a human click.

    1. Intake and screenWebhook → Code → If

      Normalises the lead, then drops obvious junk into a rejection log before a single AI call is paid for.

    2. Match prior contactData table

      Looks up history, summarises it, upserts the record. A third submission from the same buyer is known to be a third submission.

    3. Score and draftExecute Workflow · 16 nodes

      The shared scorer: retrieves brand-knowledge chunks and recorded human edits from a vector store, scores against a rubric, then drafts a grounded reply with real products, real bulk terms and real lead times.

    4. Validate, repair once, fail safeneeds_triage

      The scorer checks its own output and repairs it one time. If it still does not hold, the lead goes to needs_triage for a person — it never ships a guess.

    5. Approve and sendSingle-use token

      The approval link works exactly once. Replays are refused, expired links are refused, and only then does Gmail send — recording message and thread ids on the way out.

    6. Learn from the editData table

      If the operator edited the draft before approving, that edit is captured as a style example and retrieved by later drafts. The loop closes.

    7. Replies and follow-upsGmail Trigger + hourly schedule

      Two clocks in one workflow: inbound replies are matched to their sent thread and stop the sequence; an hourly sweep re-scores anyone silent for three days and queues a follow-up through the same approval gate.

    The decision that makes it work. One scorer, two callers — intake and the follow-up sweep both execute the same sub-workflow, so a follow-up is judged by the same brain that judged first contact. The top lead it has scored, 98/100, was escalated rather than answered: the engraving lead time did not fit the customer's delivery date, and that is a founder's call, not a draft's.

    Lead names, addresses and the brand's catalogue are withheld. Scores, rubric behaviour and the gate are the real ones.

  4. Send a stock name to a Telegram bot and get a live chart plus a full technical read before you have put the phone down.

    1. Telegram triggerMessage

      Wakes on your message and keeps the thread's memory, so you can keep asking in the same chat.

    2. Decide what is neededAI Agent · Gemini

      The agent picks its own tools for the question asked rather than running a fixed script.

    3. Search live newsTavily

      So the read is about this week, not the training data.

    4. Chart as a toolTradingView → Telegram

      The chart tool posts straight into the chat while the agent is still thinking. The user sees something within seconds instead of watching a typing dot.

    5. Return the analysisTelegram

      Trend, support and resistance, RSI, the news that matters and the risk.

    The decision that makes it work. Giving a tool a side effect. The chart is not waiting for the answer to be finished — it ships mid-thought, which is the whole difference between a bot that feels fast and one that feels broken.

  5. Instagram and YouTube in one dashboard, with no server, no database bill and no rate limit to hit.

    1. Collect on a scheduleEvery 30 minutes

      Pulls Instagram account data, daily insights, follower demographics and recent media; YouTube channel stats, the uploads playlist, video stats and two Analytics reports.

    2. ReconcileCode

      Two APIs that agree on nothing are reshaped into one record with a fixed contract.

    3. Write a snapshotData table

      One row to read instantly, plus one row of history a day so the trend survives.

    4. Serve from the snapshotWebhook · fixed JSON

      The dashboard reads the stored row, never the platform APIs. It loads instantly and cannot be rate limited by a visitor refreshing it.

    The decision that makes it work. Separating collection from serving. Most dashboards call the API when you open them, which is exactly when the API says no. Here the expensive work runs on a clock nobody is watching, and the front end only ever reads one row.

  7. A viewer comments the keyword under a reel; this decides which link belongs to that reel and sends it back, in one round trip.

    1. Link requestWebhook · POST

      ManyChat asks: this viewer, this video, this comment.

    2. Clean the requestSet

      Trims and lowercases the video name, carries the viewer's id and username, and sets the fallback link.

    3. Look up the linkData table · get

      One row per video. No AI, because there is nothing here to reason about.

    4. Log the commenterData table · insert

      Captured as a lead with their first comment — and set to continue on error, so a logging failure can never cost the viewer their reply.

    5. Reply with the right linkRespond to Webhook

      The matched link, or the profile link when a video has none. Nobody gets a dead DM.

    The decision that makes it work. Knowing when not to use AI. Five nodes, a lookup table and a fallback — and the only two failure modes that existed were designed out rather than monitored.
